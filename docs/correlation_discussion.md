# Correlation and Causation Discussion

## Observed Relationship

The analysis identified a strong positive linear association between
quantity sold and total revenue, with Pearson's correlation coefficient
of 0.7425. The coefficient of determination (R²) is 0.5513, indicating
that approximately 55.13% of the variation in revenue is explained by
its linear relationship with quantity sold in this sample.

Although the results show that transactions involving higher quantities
tend to have higher revenue, correlation alone does not establish that
quantity causes revenue to increase. The relationship must be interpreted
with consideration of other factors that may influence the variables.

## Potential Confounding Variables

Several additional factors may influence both quantity sold and revenue:

1. **Product type and unit price:** Different chocolate products may
   have different prices. Customers purchasing premium products may
   generate higher revenue even when purchasing fewer units.

2. **Discounts and promotional campaigns:** Discounts may encourage
   customers to purchase larger quantities while reducing the revenue
   earned per unit. Therefore, promotional activity can influence both
   quantity and revenue.

3. **Seasonal and holiday demand:** Events such as holidays or seasonal
   sales may increase purchase quantities. At the same time, special
   promotional pricing or changes in product mix may affect total revenue.

4. **Customer and store characteristics:** Customer preferences,
   purchasing power, store location, and store type may influence both
   the quantity purchased and the revenue generated.

These factors could contribute to the observed association and are not
fully controlled for in the simple correlation analysis.

## Reverse Causality

In the context of a transaction, quantity sold is logically one component
of the calculation of total revenue, together with unit price and any
applicable discount. Thus, purchasing more units can contribute directly
to higher transaction revenue, assuming other pricing factors remain
constant.

However, the correlation analysis itself does not establish the direction
of causality. In other research contexts, reverse causality may occur
when an outcome also influences the explanatory variable. Therefore,
the direction of the relationship should not be inferred from correlation
alone.

## Observational Study and Limitations

This project uses observational transaction data rather than a controlled
experiment. The researcher does not randomly assign customers to purchase
different quantities or control all pricing, promotional, product, and
customer-related factors. Consequently, the analysis identifies an
association but cannot isolate the independent causal effect of quantity
on revenue.

The results should therefore be interpreted as evidence of a strong
positive linear association, not definitive proof of causation. Further
analysis using multiple regression with relevant explanatory variables,
or a suitable controlled experimental design, would be needed to
investigate causal relationships more rigorously.

## Conclusion

The correlation between quantity and revenue is strong and positive,
but correlation does not necessarily imply causation. Differences in
unit prices, discounts, seasonal promotions, product types, and customer
characteristics may also influence the observed relationship. Since the
data come from an observational study, causal conclusions cannot be
established from Pearson's correlation coefficient alone.
